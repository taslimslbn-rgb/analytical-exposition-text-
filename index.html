What is an Analytical Exposition Text?

An analytical exposition text is used to persuade listeners/readers that an issue is important and needs attention, by presenting a position supported by logical arguments. In this lab, the issues discussed are Marine Plastic Pollution and Deforestation.

Generic Structure

THESISPosition / main idea
→
ARGUMENTSEvidence & reasoning
→
REITERATIONRestated conclusion

Key Expressions for Thesis Statement

These expressions usually signal that a sentence is a thesis statement:

In my opinion... I strongly believe that... It is critical that... Therefore, we must...

Example: "I strongly believe that marine plastic pollution must be stopped immediately." — this is a thesis statement, not an argument or reiteration.

📖 Example Text: Marine Plastic Pollution & Deforestation

Read this multimodal script carefully — the highlighted sentences also appear in the Simulation and Worksheet activities for Meeting 1.

[Thesis] "I strongly believe that marine plastic pollution must be stopped immediately."

[Argument] Every year, millions of tons of plastic waste enter the ocean and kill marine animals. Sea turtles mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish, and seabirds accidentally feed plastic fragments to their chicks. Scientists warn that by 2050, the ocean could contain more plastic than fish by weight.

[Reiteration] "Therefore, everyone must take action now to reduce plastic waste before it is too late."

[Thesis] "It is critical that we protect our forests from illegal logging."

[Argument] Deforestation destroys the natural habitat of countless species and speeds up climate change. Every minute, forests the size of many football fields disappear, releasing carbon dioxide that was once safely stored in the trees.

[Reiteration] "In conclusion, protecting our forests is not optional — it is our shared responsibility."

Analyzing Arguments: Evidence & Reasoning

An argument in an analytical exposition text is built from two parts: evidence (factual data, statistics, or examples) and reasoning (the explanation of why that evidence supports the thesis). In this meeting, the issues discussed are Social Media Addiction and E-waste Management.

Evidence vs. Reasoning — Example

Evidence: "Millions of tons of e-waste are dumped every year."

Reasoning: "...this shows current disposal methods are unsustainable, so manufacturers must take responsibility."

Constructing Your Own Thesis Statement

Choose one issue (Marine Plastic Pollution, Deforestation, Social Media Addiction, or E-waste Management), then write:

  • A thesis statement using one key expression.
  • Two supporting arguments (evidence + reasoning).
  • A short reiteration/conclusion that restates your position.

📖 Example Text: Social Media Addiction & E-waste Management

Read this multimodal script carefully — the highlighted sentences also appear in the Simulation and Worksheet activities for Meeting 2.

[Thesis] "I strongly believe that social media addiction must be addressed by schools and parents."

[Evidence] "Studies show that excessive social media use is linked to higher anxiety levels among teenagers."

[Reasoning] "This happens because constant comparison with others online lowers self-esteem." Teenagers spend hours scrolling through carefully curated images of other people's lives, which can create unrealistic expectations and feelings of inadequacy.

[Thesis] "It is critical that we manage e-waste responsibly."

[Evidence] "Millions of tons of e-waste are dumped every year."

[Reasoning] "This shows that current disposal methods are unsustainable." Toxic materials such as lead and mercury leak into the soil and water, harming both human health and the environment.

💡 In the Simulation tab, you will classify the highlighted sentences above as Thesis / Evidence / Reasoning. In the Worksheet, several questions are based directly on this text.
